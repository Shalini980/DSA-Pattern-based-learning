Product Except Self → left array + right array

I want you to recognize the structure underneath it.

1. First, what did you REALLY discover?

Suppose:

[1, 2, 3, 4]

For index 2, the answer is:

1 × 2 × 4

Notice something?

The answer for an element can be split into two independent parts:

elements BEFORE i × elements AFTER i

So:

answer[i]
=
(product of everything to the LEFT)
×
(product of everything to the RIGHT)

That's the actual pattern.

You used:

left[i]  = product of elements before i
right[i] = product of elements after i

and then:

answer[i] = left[i] * right[i]
THAT is what you should remember.

Not:

"There was zero, so I used two arrays."

Zero explains why division isn't safe, but zero is not the fundamental pattern.


How do you recognize this in a completely different problem?

This is the question you should ask yourself:

"For the answer at index i, do I need information from everything before i and everything after i?"

If YES → 🚨 Think prefix/suffix.

The operation doesn't even have to be multiplication.

It could be:

sum
product
minimum
maximum
XOR
count
some other associative information

For example:

Problem A

For every index, find the sum of all elements except itself.

Immediately:

answer[i] =
sum(left of i) + sum(right of i)

That's the same pattern.

Problem B

For every building, calculate something based on the tallest building to its left and tallest building to its right.

You should think:

leftMax[i]
rightMax[i]

🚨 Same underlying pattern.

This is the idea behind problems such as Trapping Rain Water.

Problem C

Imagine:

For every element, calculate the number of smaller elements on its left and the number of smaller elements on its right.

Again:

information from left
+
information from right

You're looking for ways to maintain prefix/suffix information.
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------LC 128 — Longest Consecutive Sequence

Trigger: "longest consecutive sequence" + array order doesn't matter.

Think: Hash Set.

Start: x-1 doesn't exist → x is a sequence start.

Extend: keep checking x+1.

Avoid TLE: iterate over unique values, not the original array with duplicates.

Complexity: average O(n) time, O(n) space.

And here's the important distinction for your overall DSA learning:

Don't try to remember the exact wording of LC 128.

Try to remember the question hidden underneath the wording:

"I have unordered numbers. I need the longest chain of consecutive values. How can I quickly check whether neighboring values exist?"

That question naturally leads you to:
Hash Set → sequence starts → expansion.
